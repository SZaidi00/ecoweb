About & Methodology

Food Web Explorer turns published ecological research into an interactive atlas of ecosystems. This page explains how each web is built, why the data looks the way it does, and where every dataset comes from.

Why "Salmon" isn't one species

Real food-web studies rarely measure interactions species-by-species. Researchers pool organisms that play the same feeding role into functional groups. Our "Salmon" node represents several salmon species (Chinook, Coho, Pink, Sockeye) exactly as the source study aggregated them — because diet data ("seals eat salmon") almost never exists at the resolution "seals eat 60% Chinook, 30% Coho."

We never invent resolution the data doesn't have. Where a study distinguishes life stages (salmon fry vs. adults have different predators), we keep those as separate nodes. Where it pools, we pool — and say so.

Where the data comes from

Webs are drawn from peer-reviewed, published food-web datasets. Each carries a provenance badge — Empirical study (a single published dataset) or Curated composite (assembled from diet literature, always labeled). Sources for the current build:

How to read the visualization

The vertical layout follows trophic level: producers and detritus at the bottom, apex predators at the top — energy flows upward. Line thickness shows each food source's share of a consumer's diet; dashed lines mark relationships the source study reported qualitatively, without measured flows.

What the removal simulation does — and doesn't — claim

When you remove a species, diet loss propagates through the web: a consumer losing more than 70% of its food supply collapses, which can trigger further waves. Prey released from predation are shown as "released." This is a structural dependency model — it shows who is connected to whom, and how strongly. It does not predict real population numbers, and it ignores behaviors like prey-switching. We label it that way, always.

Why webs are kept small

Each ecosystem is curated to 15–25 nodes. Network-visualization research shows graph readability degrades past a few dozen nodes; depth here comes from layers (biome → ecosystem → species), not density.
